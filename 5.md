# 5. Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors
## 5.1 Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues
### Definition:
An eigenvector of an n×n matrix A is a nonzero vector $\vec{x}$ such that $A\vec{x}=\lambda\vec{x}$ for some scalar $\lambda$.
A scalar $\lambda$ is called an eigenvalue of A if there is a nonzero solution $\vec{x}$ of $A\vec{x}=\lambda\vec{x}$; such an $\vec{x}$ is called an eigenvector corresponding to $\lambda$.

### Theorem 1:
The eigenvalues of a triangular matrix are the entries on its main diagonal.

### Theorem 2:
If $\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2, ..., \vec{v}_p$ are eigenvectors that correspond to distinct eigenvalues of an n×n matrix A, then the set $\{\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2, ..., \vec{v}_p\}$ is linearly independent.

## 5.2 The Characteristic Equation
### Theorem (The Invertible Matrix Theorem, continued):
Let A be an n×n matrix. Then A is invertible if and only if:
- s. The number zero is not an eigenvalue of A.
- t. The determinant of A is not zero.

### Theorem 3: Properties of Determinants
Let A and B be n×n matrices.
- a.$A$ is invertible if and only if $\text{det A}\neq 0$
- b. $\det(AB) = (\det A)(\det B)$
- c. $\det(A^T) = \det A$
- d. If A is triangular, then $\det A$ is the product of the entries on the main diagonal of A.
- e. 
    Row replacement operations on A do not change the determinant. 
    A row interchange changes the sign of the determinant. 
    A row scaling also scales the determinant by the same scalar factor.

### Characteristic Equation:
A scalar $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of an n×n matrix A if and only if it satisfies the characteristic equation:
$$\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$$

### Theorem 4:
If n×n matrices A and B are similar, then they have the same characteristic polynomial and hence the same eigenvalues (with the same multiplicities).

### Definitions:
- If A is an n×n matrix, then $\det(A - \lambda I)$ is a polynomial of degree n called the characteristic polynomial of A.
- The (algebraic) multiplicity of an eigenvalue $\lambda$ is its multiplicity as a root of the characteristic equation.

### Definition: Similarity
- Two n×n matrices A and B are similar if there exists an invertible matrix P such that $P^{-1}AP = B$, or equivalently, $A = PBP^{-1}$.
- Changing A to $P^{-1}AP$ is called a similarity transformation.

## 5.3  Diagonalization
### Definition: Diagonalizable
A square matrix A is **diagonalizable** if A is similar to a diagonal matrix.

### Theorem 5 (The Diagonalization Theorem):
An n×n matrix A is diagonalizable if and only if A has n linearly independent eigenvectors. 
In fact, $A = PDP^{-1}$, with D a diagonal matrix, if and only if the columns of P are n linearly independent eigenvectors of A. 
In this case, the diagonal entries of D are eigenvalues of A that correspond, respectively, to the eigenvectors in P.
#### proof idea
because $AP = PD$,and $P$ is invertible, so $A=PDP^{-1}$


### Corollary:
A is diagonalizable if and only if there are enough eigenvectors to form a basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$. Such a basis is an eigenvector basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$.

### Theorem 6:
An n×n matrix with n distinct eigenvalues is diagonalizable.

### Theorem 7:
Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ matrix whose distinct eigenvectors are $\lambda_1\dots\lambda_p$:
a. For 1 ≤ k ≤ p, the dimension of the eigenspace for $\lambda_k$ is less than or equal to the multiplicity of the eigenvalue $\lambda_k$.
b. The matrix A is diagonalizable if and only if:
   (i) the characteristic polynomial factors completely into linear factors;
   (ii) the dimension of the eigenspace for each eigenvalue $\lambda_k$ equals the multiplicity of $\lambda_k$.
c. If A is diagonalizable and $B_k$ is a basis for the eigenspace corresponding to $\lambda_k$ for each k, then the total collection of vectors in the sets $B_1, B_2, ..., B_p$ is an eigenvector basis for $\mathbb{R}^n$.

## 5.4 Eigenvectors and Linear Transformation


### Setup
- Let $ V $ be an $ n $-dimensional vector space, let $ W $ be an $ m $-dimensional vector space, and let $ T $ be any linear transformation from $ V $ to $ W $.
- We choose bases $ B $ (for $ V $) and $ C $ (for $ W $) respectively.


### Coordinate Vectors & Matrix Representation of $ T $
1. For any $ \vec{x} \in V $:
   - The coordinate vector $ [\vec{x}]_B \in \mathbb{R}^n $;
   - The coordinate vector of its image $ [T(\vec{x})]_C \in \mathbb{R}^m $.

2. Let $\{\vec{b}_1, \dots, \vec{b}_n\} $ be the basis $ B $ for $ V $:
   - If $ \vec{x} = r_1\vec{b}_1 + \cdots + r_n\vec{b}_n $, then $ [\vec{x}]_B = \begin{bmatrix}r_1\\\vdots\\r_n\end{bmatrix} $.
   - By linearity of $T$: $ T(\vec{x}) = r_1T(\vec{b}_1) + \cdots + r_nT(\vec{b}_n) $.
   - By linearity of the coordinate mapping  from $ W $ to $ \mathbb{R}^m $:
     $$[T(\vec{x})]_C = r_1[T(\vec{b}_1)]_C + \cdots + r_n[T(\vec{b}_n)]_C$$

3. Matrix relation:
   - $ [T(\vec{x})]_C = M [\vec{x}]_B $, where the matrix
     $$M = \left[ [T(\vec{b}_1)]_C \quad [T(\vec{b}_2)]_C \quad \cdots \quad [T(\vec{b}_n)]_C \right]$$
   - $ M $ is a matrix representation of $ T $, called **the matrix for $ T $ relative to the bases $ B $ and $ C $**.



### Special Case: B-matrix for $ T $
If $ W = V $ and the basis $ C = B $:
- The matrix $ M $ is called **the matrix for $ T $ relative to $ B $** (or simply the $ B $-matrix for $ T $), denoted by $ [T]_B = M $.


### Theorem 8: Diagonal Matrix Representation
- Suppose $ A = PDP^{-1} $, where $ D $ is an $ n \times n $ diagonal matrix.
- If $ B $ is the basis for $ \mathbb{R}^n $ formed from the columns of $ P $, then $ D $ is the $ B $-matrix for the transformation $ \vec{x} \mapsto A\vec{x} $.


### Auxiliary Transformation Relation & Note
1. Transformation diagram:
$$
\vec{x} \xrightarrow{A} T(\vec{x})
$$
$$
\downarrow P^{-1} \quad \quad \uparrow P
$$
$$
[\vec{x}]_B \xrightarrow{B} [T(\vec{x})]_B
$$

2. Derivation:
   - From $ A = PBP^{-1} $, we get $ B = P^{-1}AP $.

3. (note.) Method to find $ B = P^{-1}AP $:
   - Perform row operations on the block matrix $ [P \quad AP] $ to transform it into $ [I \quad P^{-1}AP] $.


## 5.5 Complex Eigenvalues
### Definition:
A complex scalar $\lambda$ satisfies $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$ if and only if there is a nonzero vector $\vec{x}$ in $\mathbb{C}^n$ such that $A\vec{x} = \lambda\vec{x}$. We call $\lambda$ a complex eigenvalue and $\vec{x}$ a complex eigenvector corresponding to $\lambda$.

### Complex Conjugate Vector:
The complex conjugate of a vector $\vec{x}$ in $\mathbb{C}^n$ is the vector $\bar{\vec{x}}$ whose entries are the complex conjugates of the entries in $\vec{x}$.

### Real and Imaginary Parts of Complex Vector:
The real and imaginary parts of a complex vector $\vec{x}$ are the vectors $\text{Re}\vec{x}$ and $\text{Im}\vec{x}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ formed from the real and imaginary parts of the entries of $\vec{x}$.

### Property of conjugates for complex matrix algebra
- $\bar{r\vec{x}}=\bar{r}\bar{\vec{x}}$
- $\bar{B\vec{x}}=\bar{B}\bar{\vec{x}}$
- $\bar{BC}=\bar{B}\bar{C}$
- $\bar{rB}=\bar{r}\bar{B}$


### Property of Complex Eigenvalues (Real Matrix):
When A is real, its complex eigenvalues and eigenvectors occur in conjugate pairs (i.e., if $\lambda = a + bi$ is an eigenvalue, then $\bar{\lambda} = a - bi$ is also an eigenvalue).

## 5.6 Discrete Dynamical Systems
### Setup:
Suppose $A$ is diagonalizable, with n linearly independent eigenvectors $\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2, ..., \vec{v}_n$ and corresponding eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, ..., \lambda_n$. Suppose the eigenvectors are arranged so that $|\lambda_1| \geq |\lambda_2| \geq \cdots \geq |\lambda_n|$.

### Solution to Recurrence Relation:
Since $\{\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2, ..., \vec{v}_n\}$ forms a basis for $\mathbb{R}^n$, any initial vector $\vec{x}_0$ can be written uniquely as $\vec{x}_0 = c_1\vec{v}_1 + c_2\vec{v}_2 + \cdots + c_n\vec{v}_n$. Then:
$$\vec{x}_1 = A\vec{x}_0 = c_1\lambda_1\vec{v}_1 + \cdots + c_n\lambda_n\vec{v}_n$$
$$\dots$$
$$\vec{x}_k = c_1(\lambda_1)^k\vec{v}_1 + \cdots + c_n(\lambda_n)^k\vec{v}_n$$

### Graphical Description of Solutions (2D Case):
The graph of $\vec{x_0},\vec{x_1}\dots$ is a trajectory of the dynamical system.
Suppose $\vec{x_k}=c_1(\lambda_1)^k\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}+c_2(\lambda_2)^k\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}$
1. $|\lambda_1| < 1$ and $|\lambda_2| < 1$: origin is an attractor.
2. $|\lambda_1| > 1$ and $|\lambda_2| > 1$: origin is a repeller.
3. $|\lambda_1| > 1$ and $|\lambda_2| < 1$: origin is a saddle point.

### Change of Variable:
The eigenvectors of A form a basis $\{\vec{v}_1, ..., \vec{v}_n\}$ for $\mathbb{R}^n$. Let $P = (\vec{v}_1 \cdots \vec{v}_n)$ and let D be the diagonal matrix with the corresponding eigenvalues on the diagonal. Given a sequence $\{\vec{x}_k\}$ satisfying $\vec{x}_{k+1} = A\vec{x}_k$, define a new sequence $\{\vec{y}_k\}$ by:
$$\vec{y}_k = P^{-1}\vec{x}_k \quad \text{or} \quad \vec{x}_k = P\vec{y}_k$$
Using $A = PDP^{-1}$, we find that:
$$P\vec{y}_{k+1} = A\vec{x}_k = AP\vec{y}_k = PDP^{-1}P\vec{y}_k = PD\vec{y}_k$$
Dividing both sides by P (since P is invertible):
$$\vec{y}_{k+1} = D\vec{y}_k$$

### Power of Matrix A:
$$A^k = PD^kP^{-1}$$
