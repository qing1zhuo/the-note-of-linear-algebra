
# 3. Determinants
## 3.1. Introduction to Determinants
### Definition:
For $n \ge 2$, the determinants of an n×n matrix $A = [a_{ij}]$ is the sum of n terms of the form $\pm a_{1j}\det A_{1j}$, with plus and minus signs alternating where the entries $$a_{11}, a_{12}, \dots, a_{1n}$$ are fixed from the first row of A. In symbols: 
$$
\det A = a_{11}\det A_{11} - a_{12}\det A_{12} + \dots + (-1)^{1+n}a_{1n}\det A_{1n}
$$


Given $A = [a_{ij}]$, the$ (i,j)$-cofactor of A is the number given by:
$$
C_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j}\det A_{ij}
$$
 Then
$$
\det A = a_{11} C_{11} + a_{12} C_{12} + \dots + a_{1n} C_{1n}
$$

### Theorem 1:
The determinant of an n×n matrix A can be computed by a cofactor expansion across any row or down any column.
The expansion across the i-th row using the cofactor is 
$$
\det A = a_{i1}C_{i1} + a_{i2}C_{i2} + \dots + a_{in}C_{in}
$$
The cofactor expansion down the j-th column is ​
$$
\det A = a_{1j}C_{1j} + a_{2j}C_{2j} + \dots + a_{nj}C_{nj}
$$

### Theorem 2:
If A is a triangular matrix, then $$\det A$$ is the product of the entries on the main diagonal of A.


## 3.2. Properties of Determinants
### Theorem 3: Row Operation
Let A be a square matrix
	a. If a multiple of one row of A is added to another row to produce a matrix B, then $$\det A = \det B$$
	b. If two rows of A are interchanged to produce B, then $$\det B = -\det A$$
	c. If one row of A is multiplied by k to produce B, then $$\det B = k\det A$$

### The connection between A and U

Suppose a square matrix A has been reduced to an echelon form U. If there are r interchanges, then :
$$
\det A = (-1)^r \det U
$$


so:
$$
\det A = \begin{cases} (-1)^r \det U = (-1)^r \left( \text{product of pivots in } U \right),\ &\text{when } A \text{ is invertible} \\ 0,\ &\text{when } A \text{ is not invertible} \end{cases}
$$


### Elementary Matrix Determinant:
If $$E$$ is an n×n elementary matrix, then
$$ \det E = \begin{cases}
1,\ &\text{if } E \text{ is a row replacement} \\
-1,\ &\text{if } E \text{ is an interchange} \\
r,\ &\text{if } E \text{ is a scale by } r
\end{cases} $$

### Theorem 4:
A square matrix A is invertible if and only if $$\det A \neq 0$$

### Theorem 5:
If A is an n×n matrix, then $$\det A^T = \det A$$

### Theorem 6: Multiplicative Property
If A and B are n×n matrices, then $\det(AB) = (\det A)(\det B)$

### Linearity property of the determinant function
Suppose that the j-th column of A is allowed to vary, and write $$A = [a_1\ \dots\ a_{j-1}\ x\ a_{j+1}\ \dots\ a_n]$$
Define a transformation $$T: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$$ by $$T(\mathbf{x}) = \det[a_1\ \dots\ a_{j-1}\ \mathbf{x}\ a_{j+1}\ \dots\ a_n]$$
Then:

- $$T(c\mathbf{x}) = cT(\mathbf{x})$$, for all scalars c and all $$\mathbf{x}$$ in $$\mathbb{R}^n$$
- $$T(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = T(\mathbf{u}) + T(\mathbf{v})$$, for all $$\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}$$ in $$\mathbb{R}^n$$


## 3.3. Cramer's rule, volume, and linear transformations
### Theorem 7: Cramer's Rule
Let A be an invertible n×n matrix. For any $$\mathbf{b}$$ in $$\mathbb{R}^n$$, the unique solution $$\mathbf{x}$$ of $$A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$$ has entries given by 
$$
x_j = \frac{\det A_j(\mathbf{b})}{\det A}
$$
where $$A_j(\mathbf{b})$$ is the matrix obtained by replacing the j-th column of A with $$\mathbf{b}$$.

### Theorem 8: An Inverse Formula
Let A be an invertible n×n matrix. Then:
$$ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det A}\text{adj }A $$

so
$$ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det A}\begin{bmatrix}
C_{11} & C_{21} & \dots & C_{n1} \\
C_{12} & C_{22} & \dots & C_{n2} \\
\vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\
C_{1n} & C_{2n} & \dots & C_{nn}
\end{bmatrix} $$

#### Explanation of Theorem 8:
For an invertible n×n matrix, the j-th column of $$A^{-1}$$ is a vector $$\mathbf{x}$$ that satisfies 
$$
A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{e}_j​
$$
 where $\mathbf{e}_j$ is the j-th column of the identity matrix. By Cramer's Rule, the i-th entry of $\mathbf{x}$ is $\frac{\det A_i(\mathbf{e}_j)}{\det A}$, which is $\frac{C_{ji}}{\det A}$ (since $$\det A_i(\mathbf{e}_j) = C_{ji}$$). So $$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det A}[C_{ji}] = \frac{1}{\det A}\text{adj }A$$

### Theorem 9:
- If A is a 2×2 matrix, the area of the parallelogram determined by the columns of A is $$|\det A|$$
- If A is a 3×3 matrix, the volume of the parallelepiped determined by the columns of A is ($$|\det A|$$)
- Let ($$\mathbf{a}_1$$) and ($$\mathbf{a}_2$$) be nonzero vectors. Then for any scalar c, the area of the parallelogram determined by ($$\mathbf{a}_1$$) and ($$\mathbf{a}_2$$) equals the area of the parallelogram determined by ($$\mathbf{a}_1$$) and ($$\mathbf{a}_2 + c\mathbf{a}_1$$)

### Theorem 10:
Let ($$T: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$$) be a linear transformation with standard matrix A. If S is a parallelogram in ($$\mathbb{R}^2$$), then
$$
\text{Area of } T(S) = |\det A| \times \text{Area of } S
$$
Let ($$T: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^3$$) be a linear transformation with standard matrix A. If S is a parallelepiped in ($$\mathbb{R}^3$$), then
$$
\text{Volume of } T(S) = |\det A| \times \text{Volume of } S
$$
