# 6. Orthogonality and Least Squares
## 6.1. Inner Product, Length, and Orthogonality
### Inner Product (Dot Product)
If $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$ are vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$, the number $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}$ is called the inner product / dot product of $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$.

### Theorem 1

$$
\text{a.  }\vec{u}·\vec{v}=\vec{v}·\vec{u}\\
\text{b.  }\vec{u} \cdot (\vec{v} + \vec{w}) = \vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} + \vec{u} \cdot \vec{w}\\
\text{c.  }(c\vec{u}) \cdot \vec{v} = c(\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}) = \vec{u} \cdot (c\vec{v})\\
\text{d.  }\vec{u} \cdot \vec{u} \geq 0, \text{ and }\vec{u} \cdot \vec{u} = 0 \text{ if and only if } \vec{u} = \vec{0}
\\
(c_1\vec{u_1}\dots c_p\vec{u_p})·\vec{w}=c_1(\vec{u_1}·\vec{w})\dots c_p(\vec{u_p}·\vec{w})
$$

### Definition: Length/Norm
The length (or norm) of a vector $\vec{v}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is the nonnegative scaler $\|\vec{v}\|$, defined by:
$$
\|\vec{v}\| = \sqrt{\vec{v} \cdot \vec{v}} = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + \cdots + v_n^2}
$$
and then
$$
\|\vec{v}\|^2=\vec{v}·\vec{v}\\\|c\vec{v}\|=|c|\|\vec{v}\|
$$

### Definition: Unit Vector and Normalize
A vector whose length is 1 is a unit vector. The process of creating a unit vector from $\vec{v}$ is normalizing $\vec{v}$, and the unit vector is in the same direction as $\vec{v}$.
$$
\vec{u}=\vec{v}/\|\vec{v}\|
$$

### Definition: Distance
For $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$, the distance between $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$, written as $\text{dist}(\vec{u}, \vec{v})$, is the length of the vector $\vec{u} - \vec{v}$:
$$
\text{dist}(\vec{u}, \vec{v}) = \|\vec{u} - \vec{v}\|
$$


### Definition: Orthogonal
Two vectors $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ are said to be orthogonal (or perpendicular) if $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = 0$.

### Theorem 2: The Pythagorean Theorem
Two vectors $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$ are orthogonal if and only if:
$$
\|\vec{u} + \vec{v}\|^2 = \|\vec{u}\|^2 + \|\vec{v}\|^2
$$


### Orthogonal Complement
If a vector $\vec{z}$ is orthogonal to every vector in a subspace $W$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$, then $\vec{z}$ is said to be orthogonal to $W$. The set of all such vectors $\vec{z}$ is called the orthogonal complement of $W$, denoted by $W^\perp$:
$$
W^\perp = \{\vec{z} \in \mathbb{R}^n \mid \vec{z} \cdot \vec{w} = 0 \text{ for all } \vec{w} \in W\}
$$


#### Properties of Orthogonal Complement
1. $W^\perp$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$.
2. a vector $\vec{x}$ is in $W^\perp$ if and only if $\vec{x}$ is orthogonal to every vector in a set that spans $W$.

### Theorem 3
Let $A$ be an $m \times n$ matrix. The orthogonal complement of the row space of $A$ is the null space of $A$, and the orthogonal complement of the column space of $A$ is the null space of $A^\top$:
$$
(\text{Row } A)^\perp = \text{Null } A\\(\text{Col } A)^\perp = \text{Null } A^\top
$$

## 6.2. Orthogonal Sets
### Definition: Orthogonal Set
A set of vectors $\{\vec{u}_1, \cdots, \vec{u}_p\}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is an orthogonal set if each pair of distinct vectors from the set is orthogonal. That is, $\vec{u}_i \cdot \vec{u}_j = 0$ when $i \neq j$.

### Theorem 4
If $S = \{\vec{u}_1, \cdots, \vec{u}_p\}$ is an orthogonal set of nonzero vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$, then $S$ is linearly independent and hence is a basis for the subspace spanned by $S$.

### Definition: Orthogonal Basis
An orthogonal basis for a subspace $W$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is a basis for $W$ that is also an orthogonal set.

### Theorem 5
Let $\{\vec{u}_1, \cdots, \vec{u}_p\}$ be an orthogonal basis for a subspace $W$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$. For each $\vec{y}$ in $W$, the weights in the linear combination $\vec{y} = c_1\vec{u}_1 + c_2\vec{u}_2 + \cdots + c_p\vec{u}_p$ are given by:
$$
c_i = \frac{\vec{y} \cdot \vec{u}_i}{\vec{u}_i \cdot \vec{u}_i} \quad (i = 1, 2, \cdots, p)
$$


### Orthogonal Projection onto a Vector
Given a nonzero vector $\vec{u}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$, consider decomposing a vector $\vec{y}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ into a sum of two vectors: one a multiple of $\vec{u}$, and the other orthogonal to $\vec{u}$:
$$
\vec{y} = \hat{\vec{y}} + \vec{z}
$$
where $\hat{\vec{y}} = \alpha\vec{u}$ (a scalar multiple of $\vec{u}$) and $\vec{z} = \vec{y} - \hat{\vec{y}}$ (orthogonal to $\vec{u}$).

#### Condition for Orthogonality
$\vec{z} = \vec{y} - \alpha\vec{u}$ is orthogonal to $\vec{u}$ if and only if:
$$
0 = (\vec{y} - \alpha\vec{u}) \cdot \vec{u} = \vec{y} \cdot \vec{u} - \alpha(\vec{u} \cdot \vec{u})
$$
Solving for $\alpha$ gives:
$$
\alpha = \frac{\vec{y} \cdot \vec{u}}{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{u}}
$$


#### Definitions
- The vector $\hat{\vec{y}} = \frac{\vec{y} \cdot \vec{u}}{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{u}} \vec{u}$ is the orthogonal projection of $\vec{y}$ onto $\vec{u}$.
- The vector $\vec{z} = \vec{y} - \hat{\vec{y}}$ is the component of $\vec{y}$ orthogonal to $\vec{u}$.

Sometimes $\hat{\vec{y}}$ is denoted by $\text{proj}_L \vec{y}$ and is called the orthogonal projection of $\vec{y}$ onto $L$.That is
$$
\hat{\vec{y}}=\text{proj}_L \vec{y} = \frac{\vec{y} \cdot \vec{u}}{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{u}} \vec{u}
$$


### Definition: Orthonormal Set
A set $\{\vec{u}_1, \cdots, \vec{u}_p\}$ is an orthonormal set if it is an orthogonal set of unit vectors (i.e., $\|\vec{u}_i\| = 1$ for all $i$, and $\vec{u}_i \cdot \vec{u}_j = 0$ for $i \neq j$). If $W$ is the subspace spanned by such a set, then the set is an orthonormal basis for $W$.

### Theorem 6
An $m \times n$ matrix $U$ has orthonormal columns if and only if $U^\top U = I_n$ (where $I_n$ is the $n \times n$ identity matrix).

### Theorem 7
Let $U$ be an $m \times n$ matrix with orthonormal columns, and let $\vec{x}$ and $\vec{y}$ be in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Then:
$$
a. \|U\vec{x}\| = \|\vec{x}\| \text{(preserves length)}\\
b. (U\vec{x}) \cdot (U\vec{y}) = \vec{x} \cdot \vec{y} \text{(preserves inner product)}\\
c. (U\vec{x}) \cdot (U\vec{y}) = 0 \text{ if and only if } \vec{x} \cdot \vec{y} = 0 \text{(preserves orthogonality)}
$$


### Definition: Orthogonal Matrix
An $n \times n$ matrix $U$ is an orthogonal matrix if its columns are orthonormal (equivalently, $U^\top U = I_n$ and $U U^\top = I_n$). This implies $U^\top = U^{-1}$ (the transpose is the inverse).

#### Properties of Orthogonal Matrices
1. If $U$ is an orthogonal matrix, then $U^{-1} = U^\top$.
2. $\det(U) = 1$ or $\det(U) = -1$ (absolute value of determinant is 1).
3. if $A$ and $B$ are orthogonal matrix, then AB is an orthogonal matrix.

### Definition: Orthogonal Transformation
If $U$ is an orthogonal matrix, then the linear transformation $\vec{y} = U\vec{x}$ is an orthogonal transformation. (Preserves length and orthogonality, by Theorem 7)

## 6.3. Orthogonal Projection
### Theorem 8: The Orthogonal Decomposition Theorem
Let $W$ be a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$. Then each $\vec{y}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ can be written uniquely in the form:
$$
\vec{y} = \hat{\vec{y}} + \vec{z}
$$
where $\hat{\vec{y}} \in W$ and $\vec{z} \in W^\perp$. If $\{\vec{u}_1, \cdots, \vec{u}_p\}$ is an orthogonal basis of $W$, then:
$$
\hat{\vec{y}} = \frac{\vec{y} \cdot \vec{u}_1}{\vec{u}_1 \cdot \vec{u}_1} \vec{u}_1 + \cdots + \frac{\vec{y} \cdot \vec{u}_p}{\vec{u}_p \cdot \vec{u}_p} \vec{u}_p
$$
and $\vec{z} = \vec{y} - \hat{\vec{y}}$.

#### Definition: Orthogonal Projection onto a Subspace
The vector $\hat{\vec{y}}$ in the orthogonal decomposition is called the orthogonal projection of $\vec{y}$ onto $W$, denoted $\text{proj}_W \vec{y}$.

If $\vec{y}$ is in $W=Span\{\vec{u_1}\dots \vec{u_p}\}$, then $\text{proj}_L \vec{y}=\vec{y}$.

### Theorem 9: The Best Approximation Theorem
Let $W$ be a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$, let $\vec{y}$ be any vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$, and let $\hat{\vec{y}}$ be the orthogonal projection of $\vec{y}$ onto $W$. Then $\hat{\vec{y}}$ is the closest point in $W$ to $\vec{y}$—that is:
$$
\|\vec{y} - \hat{\vec{y}}\| < \|\vec{y} - \vec{w}\|
$$
for all $\vec{w} \in W$ with $\vec{w} \neq \hat{\vec{y}}$. The vector $\hat{\vec{y}}$ is the best approximation to $\vec{y}$ by elements of $W$.

### Projection Matrix
If $W$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$ with orthogonal basis $\{\vec{u}_1, \cdots, \vec{u}_p\}$, then the orthogonal projection of $\vec{y}$ onto $W$ can be written as $\text{proj}_W \vec{y} = P\vec{y}$, where $P$ is the projection matrix:
$$
P = \vec{u}_1 \vec{u}_1^\top + \vec{u}_2 \vec{u}_2^\top + \cdots + \vec{u}_p \vec{u}_p^\top
$$
If $U = [\vec{u}_1 \quad \vec{u}_2 \quad \cdots \quad \vec{u}_p]$ (an $n \times p$ matrix with columns as the orthogonal basis), then:
$$
P = U(U^\top U)^{-1} U^\top=UU^\top
$$
(If the basis is orthonormal, $U^\top U = I_p$, so $P = U U^\top$)

## 6.4. The Gram-Schmidt Process
### Theorem 11: The Gram-Schmidt Process
Given a basis $\{\vec{x}_1, \cdots, \vec{x}_p\}$ for a nonzero subspace $W$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$, define:
$$
\vec{v}_1 = \vec{x}_1\\
\vec{v}_2 = \vec{x}_2 - \frac{\vec{x}_2 \cdot \vec{v}_1}{\vec{v}_1 \cdot \vec{v}_1} \vec{v}_1\\
\vec{v}_3 = \vec{x}_3 - \frac{\vec{x}_3 \cdot \vec{v}_1}{\vec{v}_1 \cdot \vec{v}_1} \vec{v}_1 - \frac{\vec{x}_3 \cdot \vec{v}_2}{\vec{v}_2 \cdot \vec{v}_2} \vec{v}_2\\
\vdots\\
\vec{v}_p = \vec{x}_p - \frac{\vec{x}_p \cdot \vec{v}_1}{\vec{v}_1 \cdot \vec{v}_1} \vec{v}_1 - \frac{\vec{x}_p \cdot \vec{v}_2}{\vec{v}_2 \cdot \vec{v}_2} \vec{v}_2 - \cdots - \frac{\vec{x}_p \cdot \vec{v}_{p-1}}{\vec{v}_{p-1} \cdot \vec{v}_{p-1}} \vec{v}_{p-1}
$$

Then $\{\vec{v}_1, \cdots, \vec{v}_p\}$ is an orthogonal basis for $W$, and $\text{Span}\{\vec{v}_1, \cdots, \vec{v}_k\} = \text{Span}\{\vec{x}_1, \cdots, \vec{x}_k\}$ for each $k = 1, 2, \cdots, p$.

### Theorem 12: The QR Factorization
If $A$ is an $m \times n$ matrix with linearly independent columns, then $A$ can be factored as $A = QR$, where:
- $Q$ is an $m \times n$ matrix whose columns form an orthonormal basis for $\text{Col } A$.
- $R$ is an $n \times n$ upper triangular invertible matrix with positive entries on its diagonal.

#### Relationships
$$
R = Q^\top A\\
Q^\top Q = I_n\\
R_{ii} > 0 \quad (i = 1, 2, \cdots, n)
$$

## 6.5. Least-Squares Problems
### Definition: Least-Squares Solution
Given an $m \times n$ matrix $A$ and a vector $\vec{b}$ in $\mathbb{R}^m$, a least-squares solution of $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ is a vector $\hat{\vec{x}}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ such that:
$$
\|A\hat{\vec{x}} - \vec{b}\| \leq \|A\vec{x} - \vec{b}\|
$$
for all $\vec{x}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$. The vector $A\hat{\vec{x}}$ is the orthogonal projection of $\vec{b}$ onto $\text{Col } A$, and $\|A\hat{\vec{x}} - \vec{b}\|$ is the least-squares error.

### Theorem 13
The set of least-squares solutions of $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ coincides with the nonempty set of solutions of the normal equations:
$$
A^\top A \vec{x} = A^\top \vec{b}
$$


### Theorem 14
Let $A$ be an $m \times n$ matrix. The following are equivalent:
a. The equation $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ has a unique least-squares solution for each $\vec{b}$ in $\mathbb{R}^m$.
b. The columns of $A$ are linearly independent.
c. The matrix $A^\top A$ is invertible.

When these conditions hold, the unique least-squares solution is:
$$
\hat{\vec{x}} = (A^\top A)^{-1} A^\top \vec{b}
$$


The distance from $\vec{b} \text{ to } A\vec{x}$ is the least-squares error of this approximation.

### Theorem 15
Given an $m \times n$ matrix $A$ with linearly independent columns, let $A = QR$ be a QR factorization of $A$ (as in Theorem 12). Then for each $\vec{b}$ in $\mathbb{R}^m$, the equation $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ has a unique least-squares solution given by:
$$
\hat{\vec{x}} = R^{-1} Q^\top \vec{b}
$$


#### Proof
Let $\hat{\vec{x}} = R^{-1} Q^\top \vec{b}$. Since $A = QR$, we have $A\hat{\vec{x}} = QR R^{-1} Q^\top \vec{b} = Q Q^\top \vec{b}$. By Theorem 12, the columns of $Q$ form an orthonormal basis for $\text{Col } A$, so $Q Q^\top \vec{b}$ is the orthogonal projection of $\vec{b}$ onto $\text{Col } A$. Thus, $A\hat{\vec{x}} = \text{proj}_{\text{Col } A} \vec{b}$, which shows that $\hat{\vec{x}}$ is a least-squares solution. Uniqueness follows from Theorem 14.

## 6.7.  Inner Product Spaces
### Definition: Inner Product on a Vector Space
An inner product on a vector space $V$ is a function that, to each pair of vectors $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$ in $V$, associates a real number $\langle \vec{u}, \vec{v} \rangle$ and satisfies the following axioms for all $\vec{u}, \vec{v}, \vec{w}$ in $V$ and all scalars $c$:
1. $\langle \vec{u}, \vec{v} \rangle = \langle \vec{v}, \vec{u} \rangle$ (symmetry)
2. $\langle \vec{u} + \vec{v}, \vec{w} \rangle = \langle \vec{u}, \vec{w} \rangle + \langle \vec{v}, \vec{w} \rangle$ (additivity)
3. $\langle c\vec{u}, \vec{v} \rangle = c\langle \vec{u}, \vec{v} \rangle$ (homogeneity)
4. $\langle \vec{u}, \vec{u} \rangle \geq 0$, and $\langle \vec{u}, \vec{u} \rangle = 0$ if and only if $\vec{u} = \vec{0}$ (positive definiteness)

A vector space with an inner product is an inner product space.

### Theorem 16: The Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality
For all $\vec{u}, \vec{v}$ in an inner product space $V$:
$$
|\langle \vec{u}, \vec{v} \rangle| \leq \|\vec{u}\| \|\vec{v}\|
$$
where $\|\vec{u}\| = \sqrt{\langle \vec{u}, \vec{u} \rangle}$ (norm induced by the inner product).

### Theorem 17: The Triangle Inequality

For all $\vec{u}, \vec{v}$ in an inner product space $V$:
$$
\|\vec{u} + \vec{v}\| \leq \|\vec{u}\| + \|\vec{v}\|​
$$

### Generalized Orthogonalization 

Given a basis $\{\vec{x}_1 \cdots \vec{x}_p\}$ for a nonzero subspace $W$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$, define: 
$$
\vec{v}_1 = \vec{x}_1
\\
\vec{v}_2 = \vec{x}_2 - \frac{\langle \vec{x}_2, \vec{v}_1 \rangle}{\langle \vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_1 \rangle} \vec{v}_1
\\
\vdots
\\
\vec{v}_p = \vec{x}_p - \sum_{i=1}^{p-1} \frac{\langle \vec{x}_p, \vec{v}_i \rangle}{\langle \vec{v}_i, \vec{v}_i \rangle} \vec{v}_i
$$
 Then $\{\vec{v}_1 \cdots \vec{v}_p\}$ is an orthogonal basis for $W$. 

In addition, $\text{Span}\{\vec{x}_1 \cdots \vec{x}_p\} = \text{Span}\{\vec{v}_1 \cdots \vec{v}_p\}$.
